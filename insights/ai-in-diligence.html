How I use AI in diligence, and where I don’t

Let it draft. Keep people deciding. Give every number a source.

22 September 2026 · 4 min read

Every deal team I know is now experimenting with AI. Most of the conversation is about speed. Speed matters, but it isn’t the interesting part. The interesting part is what the hours get spent on once the reading, summarizing, and cross-checking stop eating them.

Here is the rule I’ve settled on, and the places I apply it.

Where it earns its keep

  • The first read. A CIM is a sales document. A first pass that pulls out the claims, the numbers behind them, and the questions they raise is exactly the kind of structured reading machines do well.
  • The memo skeleton. Turning a pile of notes into a consistent structure (thesis, drivers, risks, open questions) means the team argues about substance, not formatting.
  • Consistency checks. Does the memo’s revenue figure match the model? Does the risk section mention the customer concentration the data room shows? Tireless and boring, which is the point.
  • Briefing seniors. A partner walking into a management meeting needs three things, not thirty. I built Deal-to-Script for exactly this.

Where I keep it out

  • Judgment calls on people. Whether to back a management team is a human decision, made in a room, with the reasoning written down.
  • The valuation conclusion. A model can be drafted with help. The number we’re willing to pay is owned by the people who will answer for it.
  • Anything confidential without approval. Client and deal information goes only into tools the firm has approved for it. That isn’t negotiable, and it shouldn’t need saying.
  • Unsourced figures. If a number can’t be traced to a page, a cell, or a filing, it doesn’t go in the memo.
AI drafts. People decide. Every number gets a source.

What actually changed

The biggest shift isn’t that work gets done faster, though it does. It’s that the first team conversation on a new opportunity now starts with the questions instead of the summary. That’s a better meeting, and it’s a better use of the most expensive hour in the process.

The failure mode to watch is subtle: fluent output that sounds right and isn’t. The guardrails above exist because fluency is cheap and accuracy isn’t. Treat the machine like a fast, eager first-year analyst. Check the work, praise the structure, and never let it present to the investment committee.
